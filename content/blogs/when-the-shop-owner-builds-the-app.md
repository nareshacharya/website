Picture a woman who runs a two-chair salon in a busy neighbourhood. Her bookings live in a paper diary and a long WhatsApp thread. On Saturdays she misses calls because her hands are full, and once a month two customers turn up for the same slot. Her nephew promised to build her a booking site last year. He means well, but he has exams.

Now imagine she types a few sentences into an AI builder one Sunday night. Two chairs, haircuts take forty minutes, colour takes two hours, closed on Tuesdays, send a reminder the day before. The tool asks whether she wants a deposit. By the time her tea is cold, she is looking at a preview of a booking page that works.

That scene is no longer far-fetched. In the last two weeks, several companies shipped tools that make it possible. The more interesting question for people who build products is what happens on day two.

![When the shop owner builds the app](/images/blog/when-the-shop-owner-builds-the-app.png)

## What shipped in November

On 10 November, Bluehost [launched AI Builder](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/bluehost-launches-ai-builder-turning-business-ideas-into-websites-and-working-apps-in-minutes-302935303.html), which lets owners describe a website or business app in plain language. The company lists appointment booking, CRM, customer portals and internal dashboards among the things it can build. The AI asks a few clarifying questions, builds a first version, and then takes changes through chat. Two details stood out to me. AI credits are spent on building and editing, not hosting, so a published site can stay online when credits run out, subject to plan terms. And the product is backed by 24/7 human support.

On 16 November, GoDaddy [introduced Airo AI Builder](https://aboutus.godaddy.net/newsroom/news-releases/press-release-details/2026/New-GoDaddy-Airo-AI-Builder-Lets-Small-Businesses-Turn-Ideas-into-Websites-and-Apps/default.aspx), which builds websites, web apps and online stores from text or voice prompts. It includes preview environments to test changes before they go live, a database with an admin panel, connectors to more than 90 tools, and the option to download your code at any time. GoDaddy says it is available in more than 150 countries with multi-language support, while the full integrated store experience is currently limited to the US and Canada.

Square went in a related direction on 12 November. Alongside its [Squaremates business agent](https://squareup.com/us/en/press/introducing-squaremates), it announced Squaremates Apps, which let sellers build and host their own apps on Square, for US sellers at launch.

## What owners actually build first

GoDaddy also shared something I found more useful than any feature list. It analysed an anonymised sample of more than 100,000 projects from a six-week beta period. Health and beauty, home and property, and business services together made up more than a third of creations. Food and travel was about one in eight. Nearly one in ten projects were rebuilds of an existing site.

![Share of classified creations in GoDaddy's Airo AI Builder beta](/images/blog/what-small-businesses-built.png)

These are company-reported numbers from one platform, so I would not treat them as a picture of the whole market. Still, the pattern makes sense. The first things owners build are the ones closest to revenue: appointments, enquiries, reservations. Nobody starts with an analytics dashboard. They start with the thing that stops the phone ringing during a haircut.

## Three ways to get software, compared fairly

For most of my 20+ years in UX and product, a small business had two realistic options. Hire someone to build it, or subscribe to a ready-made app and adapt the business to it. AI builders add a third. None of the three is simply better.

![Three ways a small business gets software, compared](/images/blog/three-ways-to-get-software.png)

A developer gives you something that fits, and a person who owns the details, but it costs more and every change means another visit. A ready-made app brings proven flows and updates, but the business bends to the tool. An AI builder is fast and cheap to try, and changes are a conversation away. Its weakness is quieter. The quality of the result depends heavily on the owner's brief and on whether anyone checks the work.

## What is genuinely good here

I want to give credit where it is due. A few product decisions in these launches show real care for the person on the other side.

Preview before publish is a big one. It gives an owner a safe place to make mistakes. The option to download your code means a business is not locked into its first choice. Separating building credits from hosting removes a real fear, that the site goes dark because a monthly allowance ran out. And keeping human support in the loop accepts a simple truth. A salon owner who gets stuck at 9 pm needs a person, not another prompt.

## What deserves a calm second look

The first version is the easy part. Day two is where small businesses will feel the difference between a demo and a product.

- **Who checks the edge cases?** What happens when two customers book the last slot at the same moment, or someone cancels an hour before? An owner rarely thinks to ask.
- **Whose data is it?** Customer names and phone numbers now sit in a database the owner created by chatting. It helps to know where it lives and how to export it.
- **Does it work for every customer?** Older customers, slow connections and screen readers are easy to forget when the preview looks good on a laptop.
- **What does it cost over a year?** Credits for edits can add up if a site changes often. That is worth a quick estimate up front.

None of this is a reason to wait. These are the same questions any product team should ask before launch. The difference is that the product team is now one person.

## The India angle

This matters a great deal in India, where small businesses carry so much of the economy. On 20 November, Microsoft India and Samhita-CGF, with Atal Innovation Mission at NITI Aayog, [held a dialogue on AI enablement for MSMEs](https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleseDetailm.aspx?PRID=2339146&lang=1&reg=1). The programme is helping 3,000 MSMEs across Andhra Pradesh, Delhi-NCR, Karnataka and Maharashtra adopt AI workflows over a 60-day learning journey. The partners unveiled a multilingual chatbot so owners can keep learning in their own language after the training ends, and they are planning a national MSME AI adoption playbook.

I like the emphasis on learning that continues after the workshop. Tools are arriving faster than habits. The businesses that benefit most will be the ones that learn to review what the AI builds, not just ask it to build.

## My take for designers, product people and consultants

When owners can build their own first version, our role does not disappear. It moves. The valuable work shifts from making screens to helping people make good decisions about what they built.

I think that looks like a few practical things. Short checklists an owner can run before publishing. A one-hour review that tests the booking flow on a cheap phone. Good default patterns built into these platforms, so a confirmation message and a cancellation policy come out right without anyone asking. Consultants who can sit with an owner for an afternoon and turn a working prototype into something trustworthy.

As a leader, I would also ask my teams to watch these tools closely, even if we never sell to small businesses. The way an owner builds today, by describing intent, previewing and refining, is the way many enterprise teams will build soon.

The salon owner does not need to become a product manager. She needs her Saturday bookings to work, and a friendly nudge to test the edge cases before her customers find them.

If you run a small business, what is the first tool you would build for yourself?

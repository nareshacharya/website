Between April 6 and October 6, 2026, selected AI design announcements pointed toward a more connected workflow: describe an idea, shape it visually, inspect the code, and keep working with a team.

The useful question for UX professionals is what these capabilities let a team learn, and what still needs human review. These selected announcements describe capabilities and release plans. They do not establish that every feature reached every user, or that faster generation improved product outcomes.

## April brought another route from a brief to a prototype

On April 17, Anthropic announced Claude Design in research preview. The launch described visual work such as designs and prototypes, with refinement through conversation and direct controls. [Anthropic's launch announcement](https://www.anthropic.com/news/claude-design-anthropic-labs)

The design implication is straightforward: more people can bring a concrete starting point to a discussion. The next responsibility is to explain what that starting point represents. Is the team comparing an idea, testing a task, or reviewing a nearly finished product?

A realistic-looking prototype can blur those stages. Label sample data and unfinished behavior so the level of polish does not become the only signal of readiness.

## June connected code and canvas more closely

On June 24, Figma announced code layers that would place interactive code on the design canvas. Its article described comparing alternatives and extracting editable designs from code. The announcement said the feature would roll out in closed beta, so it should be read as a dated capability announcement with a limited initial release. [Figma code-layer announcement](https://www.figma.com/blog/code-on-the-figma-canvas/)

This suggests a useful shift in design reviews. Teams can compare how alternatives behave as well as how they look. They still need to decide which source is current and how an approved change reaches the production product.

Without that agreement, easier movement between tools can create more versions to reconcile.

## August put measurement under the spotlight

Figma's August 28 research article described a continuing study using surveys, an impact index, and interviews to understand changes in design work. The index records reported experience and expectations. It is not a direct measurement of every product's quality. [Figma's research-method article](https://www.figma.com/blog/measuring-the-impact-of-ai/)

For an individual team, this is a reason to ask specific questions. How much time goes into reviewing generated work? Which changes survive user testing? Does the team reach a useful decision sooner?

Those questions connect AI use to the work the team actually needs to complete.

## September made generated tools more inspectable

On September 1, Figma announced sharing and code-access updates for generative plugins and shaders. The documented changes included viewing and downloading the code behind created tools. [Figma's generative-tool update](https://www.figma.com/blog/how-we-built-generative-plugins-and-shaders/)

The practical opportunity is to turn a repeated design task into a tool that can be reviewed and reused. The corresponding question is ownership: who checks the behavior, maintains the tool, and handles a failure?

Inspectability helps make that work possible. It does not complete the review by itself.

## October adds a cost decision

Figma's official FAQ schedules general availability for the Design agent and Weave tools on October 6, with use then drawing from AI credits. As of this article's source check, that is a published schedule rather than an account-level rollout test. [Figma's availability and credit FAQ](https://help.figma.com/hc/en-us/articles/42614902212887-AI-credit-updates-FAQ)

Teams adopting an AI workflow need to understand both its usefulness and the cost of repeated attempts. Include review and correction in that calculation.

## What this means for a real design task

Imagine a team redesigning an appointment-booking flow. AI could help a team explore several layouts or create a working starting point. The team still needs to check the full journey: no appointments available, an interrupted request, a changed date, and a confirmation the person understands.

A sensible review record would capture the question, the chosen tool, the version, the observed problem, and the next change. This makes the learning visible even when the tools evolve quickly.

Use faster creation to examine more meaningful options. Keep the evidence for a decision close to the design, and check current limitations before committing a team to a new workflow.
